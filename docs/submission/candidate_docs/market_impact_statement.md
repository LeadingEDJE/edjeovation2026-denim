# Market & Impact Statement

## Target Customer / Client Persona

**Primary buyer:** a retail store, field, or clienteling leader at a specialty apparel brand responsible for improving in-store conversion, associate consistency, appointment quality, and post-visit follow-up.

**Primary user:** an in-store stylist or associate running guided denim fittings during planned service windows.

The current demo is framed around Abercrombie denim appointments, with mocked store coverage for SoHo, Columbus/Easton, and Century City. Customers can book a fitting, select womens, mens, or both catalog audiences, share style goals, message the appointment thread, receive a recap, and submit feedback. Associates use a web dashboard to manage the appointment lifecycle across Open, In Progress, Completed, Cancelled, and No-show views.

Key pain points:

- Associates often enter fittings with limited context about occasion, style, fit preferences, catalog audience, or prior order behavior.
- Denim is fit-sensitive, so poor prep can create low confidence, extra fitting-room churn, missed sales, and avoidable returns.
- Store teams need a repeatable way to prepare products, reassign stylists, communicate with customers, and capture outcomes without adding heavy process.
- Retail leaders need a service model that can scale beyond one highly skilled stylist and still feel personal.

What they want is a lightweight appointment workflow that helps qualified associates show up prepared: a clear customer snapshot, AI-assisted product shortlist, product prep states, messaging, session notes, customer recap, and feedback loop.

## Business Value / ROI

All figures below are estimates or hypotheses for pilot planning only. They are not measured results from this hackathon build.

**Associate time saved:** If a prepared shortlist and rationale reduce manual browsing, rack pulling, and pre-appointment guesswork by an estimated 10-15 minutes per appointment, then a store running 4 guided fittings in a day could save roughly 40-60 minutes of associate prep time. Assumption: associates currently prepare manually using customer notes, memory, and floor knowledge.

**Higher appointment quality:** Product prep states such as Suggested, Pulled, and Skip create an operational handoff. A stylist can see what has been prepared, add notes, and avoid restarting work if another associate takes over. Assumption: guided appointments are most valuable when the customer arrives to a curated fitting room instead of a generic browsing experience.

**Conversion upside:** Better prepared appointments may increase the chance that a customer finds a preferred fit or outfit. A reasonable pilot hypothesis is that personalized prep improves appointment-to-purchase conversion compared with unprepared walk-in service. This should be validated, not assumed, by comparing appointment conversion, average order value, and units per transaction against store baselines.

**Return reduction:** Fit-aware recommendations and recaps may reduce fit-driven returns by helping customers choose styles, rises, stretch levels, and sizes with associate guidance. Assumption: a meaningful share of denim returns are driven by fit uncertainty.

**Retention and clienteling value:** Messaging threads, appointment recaps, and feedback give the store a reason to continue the relationship after the visit. This may improve repeat booking and customer confidence. Pilot metrics should include repeat appointments, feedback ratings, recap engagement, and follow-up purchases.

## Competitive Alternatives

**Manual prep:** Associates can review notes, remember customer history, and pull denim manually. This is flexible but inconsistent, especially when staffing changes or when a less experienced associate owns the appointment. The proposed workflow makes prep visible, repeatable, and easier to transfer.

**Generic clienteling tools:** CRM, appointment booking, and associate task tools can store notes and schedule visits, but they are usually not denim-fit-aware. They do not natively combine catalog audience, fit profile, order-history signals, product prep states, appointment messaging, and customer recaps into one associate workflow.

**E-commerce recommenders:** Online recommendation systems can suggest products, but they are optimized for browsing and purchase flows rather than an associate-led fitting room. They usually do not help a store team pull products, track prep status, reassign stylists, or complete an appointment lifecycle.

This solution is different because it is built around the in-store denim service moment. It connects customer intake, womens/mens catalog selection, AI-assisted product suggestions, store/stylist scheduling, messaging, product prep, recap, and feedback in one demo workflow. The AI is useful but not positioned as the service itself; the associate remains responsible for reviewing, editing, and delivering the experience.

## Why This Matters Right Now

Retailers are trying to make stores more valuable than simple transaction points. Denim is a strong use case because fit, confidence, styling context, and associate expertise matter. A guided fitting can turn a difficult category into a differentiated service experience.

The timing is also practical because AI-assisted curation has matured enough to support constrained, explainable workflows. In this product, AI can help re-rank a known product shortlist and generate rationales, while deterministic rules and associate oversight keep the experience grounded. That makes it more appropriate for a store workflow than an open-ended chatbot.

Personalization expectations are rising, but store teams still need tools that fit real operations. This demo focuses on non-peak appointment windows, three mocked stores, a scraped/synthetic Abercrombie catalog, and mocked customer/order/store/stylist data. That scope is intentionally pilot-sized: it shows how a retailer could test guided fittings, measure prep time and conversion hypotheses, and learn whether the service model deserves broader rollout before investing in production integrations.
